Bathroom Water Damage in Norman

Bathrooms are built to get wet on the surface, not underneath it. Once caulk splits, grout thins out or the toilet seal gives up, each shower and flush sends a bit of water through. It collects in the subfloor and the wall behind the tile, out of sight. Our Norman team finds where the water is getting in, measures how far it has gone, and dries or removes what is wet.

Slow leaks are the norm here, which means what you find has typically been there longer, and gone further, than it seems. Under a toilet, the subfloor may be soft from top to bottom even though nothing looks wrong with the tile. With thermal imaging and moisture meters, we map what is wet before any demolition, and we open just the sections that will not dry in place.

Warning Signs

  • On the wall beside the shower, baseboard that has swelled or paint that is peeling
  • Dark grout that never seems to dry out between showers
  • Floor tiles that sound hollow, rock underfoot, or have come loose near the tub
  • Slight rocking when you sit on the toilet, or a dark ring on the floor around it
  • A spongy feel in the floor beside the tub or in front of the vanity
  • Directly under the bathroom, a ceiling with a brown stain or a sag
Behind the Surface

Risks That Are Easy to Miss

A Failed Shower Pan

Every use of the shower sends water beneath its floor when the pan or liner is cracked. Framing under the shower stays saturated even though the surface shows no flaw.

Trapped Steam

A weak or missing exhaust fan leaves steam in the room after every shower. That moisture settles on walls and ceilings, feeds mold, and slows the drying of anything that is already wet.

Slow Vanity Drips

Months can pass with a supply valve or drain joint dripping, hidden by whatever is stored in the vanity. First the bottom of the cabinet swells, and then the wall behind and the subfloor get wet.

Damage Downstairs

The stain on the lower ceiling can be several feet off from the leak itself, because water travels along pipes and joists first. Light fixtures and wiring can be in its path.

What We Do

What Happens After You Call

1

Find the Leak

We test the tub, shower, toilet and supply lines one at a time, and use moisture meters and thermal imaging to see where water has traveled under tile and inside walls.

2

Open What Is Needed

We take out rotted subfloor, soft wall board and tile that has come loose. A toilet leak means contamination, so anything porous that will not come clean is removed.

3

Dry the Cavities

The subfloor, the framing in the walls and the space above the lower ceiling are dried with air movers and dehumidifiers. Nothing is sealed back up until we have treated with antimicrobial and the readings show dry.

4

Rebuild and Seal

We replace subfloor and backer board, and can coordinate tile, a new toilet seal, fresh caulk and ceiling repair so the leak path is closed, not covered over.

The Clock

What Happens if You Wait

The first few days

Water Gets Past

A little water passes the failed grout or caulk every time someone showers. It soaks into the backer board and the edge of the subfloor, with nothing visible yet.

The first several weeks

The Subfloor Never Dries

Showers come too often for the wood beneath the tile to ever dry. Mold gets going in the wall cavity and on the floor's underside.

One to three months

Signs Show Up

The caulk line goes black, the grout gets darker, and tiles work loose. Where a room sits underneath, its ceiling develops a stain that gradually spreads.

Six months and beyond

The Structure Suffers

Joists can begin to rot, and the subfloor near the toilet or tub becomes soft. Drying alone no longer fixes it, because carpentry and new tile are now part of the repair.

Appliance Leak Water Damage in Norman: What to Know

Most of the appliance leaks we see trace back to three machines. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. Behind a refrigerator, the thin tube feeding the ice maker may drip for months. Cleanup is different for each of them.

For homeowners in Norman, we begin by sliding the machine out to see how far the water traveled. Kitchen leaks tend to end up beneath the cabinets and underneath the floor. Laundry room water goes into the wall at the back of the machine and the neighboring rooms, and with an upstairs laundry, the ceiling underneath. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

Do This First

Before We Arrive

  1. Shut the Machine DownCancel the cycle, and pull the plug only when the floor and the outlet near it are dry. If not, switch it off at the breaker instead.
  2. Close the Supply ValveWashers have hot and cold valves on the wall behind them. Look below the sink for the valve that feeds a dishwasher. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Soak Up Standing WaterUse towels or a mop on whatever water is within reach, which keeps it from spreading beneath flooring and cabinets. Vacuums are safe for this only when built for wet and dry pickup.
  4. Leave the Door ShutA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. The entire load of water may spill across the floor if the door is opened mid-cycle.
  5. Document What FailedKeep the part that failed, whether a hose, a fitting or a valve, and get photos of it. It may be requested by the manufacturer of the appliance and by your insurance company.
Will Insurance Pay?

What About Insurance?

When a machine overflows unexpectedly or a hose bursts, that is typically treated as sudden and accidental, which many homeowners policies cover. What is typically paid is the water damage, while the failed part and the appliance are left out. Gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage, so slow seepage at a dishwasher or an ice maker line makes for a tougher claim. How old the hose was and how it was maintained may also come up with some insurers. Check your policy, keep the failed part and take photos before cleanup.

Cost

What Affects the Cost

  • Whether this was a sudden flood or slow seepage, and which machine it came from.
  • If the flooring and cabinets have to come out or can be dried where they are.

  • Type of water, since gray discharge requires sanitizing and clean supply water does not.

Why It Happens

What Causes It

  • A failed water inlet valve that keeps filling the washer or dishwasher after it should stop.
  • Water under a refrigerator from a blocked defrost drain or a drain pan with a crack in it.
  • Worn dishwasher door gaskets, cracked drain hoses or a failed pump seal.
  • Aging rubber supply hoses on a washing machine, which weaken and then split under pressure.
  • A dishwasher that was installed out of level or with a supply connection left loose.
If It Is Left

What the Water Damages

Ceiling Below an Upstairs Laundry

Water from a second floor laundry room drops through the floor and collects on the ceiling below. The drywall stains and sags, and the insulation above it stays wet.

Flooring and Subfloor

Water slides beneath laminate, vinyl plank and hardwood. The boards swell or cup, while the subfloor below remains wet well after the top looks dry.

Cabinet Damage

Dishwasher leaks soak cabinet sides and toe kicks from underneath. Swelling and separation follow, and for weeks the soaked cabinet boxes may trap moisture against the wall.

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

Drywall edges and baseboards pull up water that stands on a kitchen or laundry room floor. Often the worst of it is on the wall in back of the appliance, where it went unseen.
